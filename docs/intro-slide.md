# Slide 4. Switzerland today

## On-screen copy

# AI use is growing faster than the rules

### Accountability, data protection, and high-risk uses are still being defined.

## In everyday life

In 2025, **more than 40%** used generative AI; among 15–24 year olds, **about 80%**.

## At work

Use is spreading faster than organisational rules and training.

## In schools

AI is already present; responsibility and data protection still apply.

## In policy

Switzerland is developing targeted sector rules rather than one AI act.

## Presenter notes

This is the situation participants are entering.

AI is no longer a niche technology in Switzerland. The Federal Statistical Office reported that more than two in five people used generative AI to create content in 2025, rising to four in five among people aged 15 to 24.

At work, recent surveys reported through the federal SME portal show widespread use, but uneven organisational maturity. Tools are often adopted before governance, training, data rules, and accountability are fully settled.

Schools face a similar gap between use and institutional clarity. Existing federal and cantonal data-protection and education law already applies. Educa's legal review notes that schools generally remain responsible for the systems they procure and that some uses involving sensitive data or consequential decisions require careful case-by-case legal assessment.

Switzerland does not currently have a single overarching AI law. The Federal Council's approach is to combine existing law with targeted, sector-specific changes and non-binding measures. A consultation draft to implement the Council of Europe AI Convention is due by the end of 2026, with emphasis on transparency, data protection, non-discrimination, and supervision.

This means the workshop is taking place while practice is spreading and the rules are still being shaped.

# Slide 8. The experiment and shared outcome

## On-screen copy

# Two loops turn discussion into consensus

The human process skips the vote in Loop 1.

## Discuss · test · refine · accept

1. Discuss as a group
2. Representative shares
3. Everyone votes
4. Check consensus
5. Revise
6. Accept consensus

## Presenter notes

This workshop compares a human process with an AI-supported process for turning group input into an accepted consensus.

Loop 1 forms and tests the statements. Both processes begin with an open group discussion, followed by one representative sharing the group’s proposed statements.

The AI-supported process then adds an all-participant vote. If the vote surfaces concerns, the group can return to discussion. The human process skips the all-participant vote but still continues to Loop 2.

Loop 2 is shared by both processes: participants check the emerging consensus, revise the statements, and check again as needed.

The final step is to accept the consensus while recording any important remaining disagreement.

The shared outcome records:

### What should happen?

A clear policy direction.

### Which two conditions are essential?

The two protections without which the use should not proceed.

### What remains unresolved?

The most important disagreement or uncertainty.

Complete agreement is not required. A fair record of disagreement is a valid result.

# Slide 10. Expert findings on judgement

## On-screen copy

# AI speeds up output — and raises the bar for judgement

## The everyday case

Draft, translate, research, code, analyse.

## The judgement problem

Fluent output can still be wrong or biased.

## The accountability failure

People need time, expertise, and authority to challenge AI.

## Presenter notes

The expert focus groups described LLMs as an established part of many professional workflows rather than a future possibility.

Their strongest competence finding was that operating a tool is not enough. People need domain knowledge and critical judgement to detect omissions, weak sources, hidden errors, and plausible-sounding falsehoods.

This creates a paradox: LLMs reduce the effort required to produce text, code, summaries, and translations, but they increase the amount of material requiring evaluation.

The familiar safeguard “a human makes the final decision” may therefore become nominal if the human cannot realistically review the volume of automated output or lacks authority to reject it.

The focus groups did not frame the choice as adoption or rejection. They preferred context-sensitive use that strengthens human ability rather than replacing the thinking through which competence develops.

TA-SWISS draft, sections 3.2.1–3.2.3 and 3.3.1.

### Using AI

People may support voluntary assistance while demanding much stronger protections when AI exercises institutional power over them.

# Slide 12. Online findings about work

## On-screen copy

# Workers want support through the AI transition

## The transition is here

**71%** felt effects on work, study, or job search.

## Train people inside their current jobs

**88%** supported workplace AI training.

## Share responsibility for adaptation

**83%** wanted institutions to share responsibility.

## Watch inequality

**78%** expected AI to increase inequality.

## Presenter notes

These results describe the online respondents, not the Swiss population.

The clearest signal is conditional openness. Respondents generally expected AI to change work, but they did not want workers or the labour market to absorb the transition without support.

In the smaller follow-up vote, 9 of 16 respondents said AI had raised expectations in their field and 9 expected fewer jobs in their field over the next five years. At the same time, 9 described its overall effect on their work as somewhat positive. This combination captures the central tension: gains and pressure can arrive together.

The workplace literature reinforces the uncertainty. It focuses heavily on productivity and efficiency, while long-term causal evidence about job quality, displacement, reskilling, and how gains are distributed remains thinner.

# Slide 16. Experimental findings about learning

## On-screen copy

# AI access alone did not improve learning

LLM access alone produced no clear overall gain.

## The shortcut case

**“Do it for me.” — 56% of school prompts.**

## The reflective case

**“Help me question this.” — 29% at university; 21% in schools.**

## The metacognitive case

**“Help me examine my thinking.” — 17% at university; 1% in schools.**

### How students use AI matters more than access alone.

## Presenter notes

The experiment included 200 learners across several educational populations. Participants completed comparable writing tasks with and without access to an LLM.

For primary and secondary learners, 55.57% of prompts were generative, 21.11% reflective, and only 1.37% metacognitive. University students used fewer generative prompts and substantially more reflective and metacognitive prompts: 44.22%, 29.25%, and 17.42% respectively.

The experiment did not find a significant overall performance effect from LLM access, task order, or the brief instructional framing. It therefore does not support a simple claim that access by itself improves or damages short-term performance.

There was an exploratory indication among younger learners that reflective or metacognitive prompting may relate to better relative outcomes. The effects were small and partly only marginally significant, so this should be presented as a possible mechanism rather than a causal conclusion.

TA-SWISS draft, sections 2.5.6–2.5.9.

# Slide 18. Online findings about education

## On-screen copy

# Support grows when judgement stays visible

Participants favoured gradual use with safeguards.

## Teach judgement first

**89%:** teach students to check errors and bias.

## Grow autonomy with age

AI in graded work: **14% in primary → 39% in higher education**.

## Redesign the proof of learning

**77%:** oral checks, projects, disclosure, process evidence.

## Protect data and teacher responsibility

**91%:** train teachers. **89%:** protect student data.

## Presenter notes

These results again describe the online respondents and should not be treated as national estimates.

Participants supported adapting education to widespread AI use, but their strongest priority was not simply increasing access. It was teaching learners how to evaluate AI-generated information.

The follow-up vote asked what proportion of graded work should permit some AI use. The mean preferred share increased from 13.5% in primary education to 25.9% in secondary education, 34.3% in vocational education, and 39.3% in higher education.

Participants also favoured assessment redesign. The most common choices included oral or in-class checks, projects and applied tasks, clearer rules, disclosure of AI use, and more evidence of the student’s learning process.

The online evidence therefore suggests a conditional position: begin with foundational abilities and critical training, increase permitted use gradually, and redesign assessment so that the learner’s own understanding remains visible.

# Slide 22. Responsibility and trust

## On-screen copy

# Responsibility is shared; trust is not

## Build capability through education

**87%** gave schools and universities responsibility for AI skills.

## Employers have responsibility and low trust

**67%** gave employers responsibility; only **8%** trusted their guidance.

## Independent knowledge creates legitimacy

Universities and researchers led trust at **77%**.

## Democracy needs a shared reality

**96%** worried AI could make truth harder to establish.

## Presenter notes

The online respondents distributed responsibility across several actors rather than assigning it to individuals or government alone.

But responsibility and trust did not align. Employers were frequently assigned responsibility for AI competence, while very few respondents selected employers as a trusted source of guidance. Technology companies showed a similar gap.

Universities and researchers were the most trusted source, followed by independent journalists, civil society, consumer-protection organisations, and public authorities.

This suggests that implementation may require employers and technology companies, while public legitimacy may depend on independent institutions and transparent oversight.

The concern about truth connects competence policy to democratic resilience. The issue is not only whether individual outputs contain errors, but whether people can maintain a shared basis for public judgement.

# Workshop app and AI-support rules

Both routes must:

* use the same participant inputs;
* use the same template;
* follow the same word limits;
* produce one provisional outcome;
* introduce no new arguments;
* avoid overstating consensus;
* preserve important disagreement;
* revise only after participant feedback;
* display statements, alignment, and the provisional outcome through the participant app.

In the human process, the group discusses and one representative shares its statements. It skips the all-participant vote, then continues to the consensus check and revision loop.

In the AI-supported process, the group discusses, one representative shares its statements, and all participants vote. It can repeat this loop before continuing to the consensus check and revision loop.
